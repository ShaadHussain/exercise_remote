In many ways, South Korea was simply doing what the world’s great philanthropists, policymakers, and politicians of that time wanted. Fears about a busy, hungry world went mainstream in the sixties. The Kennedy administration publicly argued that population control was a legitimate policy focus. In 1967, President Lyndon B Johnson [asked](https://books.google.co.uk/books?id=P5svEAAAQBAJ&pg=PA227&lpg=PA227&dq=Ghandi+%E2%80%9Cto+join+a+truly+world+wide+effort+to+bring+population+and+food+production+back+into+balance%E2%80%9D&source=bl&ots=nde_iAxhTP&sig=ACfU3U0PkYfICy2VWFiPRSt5_u6jIQM-1g&hl=en&sa=X&ved=2ahUKEwjl4brjlo2HAxV7gf0HHYVrAykQ6AF6BAgPEAM#v=onepage&q&f=false) Indian Prime Minister Indira Gandhi ‘to join a truly worldwide effort to bring population and food production back into balance’. [Henry Kissinger](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve14p1/d121?) was worried that ‘excessive population growth’ would hold back economic development and social progress and would undermine US interests by creating political tensions that could lead to instability. The president of the World Bank, Robert McNamara, made family planning a [priority](https://archivesholdings.worldbank.org/records-of-president-robert-s-mcnamara) for the Bank, giving direct financing for contraception in 1970 and tying population targets to aid. John D Rockefeller III, who [founded](https://www.prb.org/resources/john-d-rockefeller-3rd-statesman-and-founder-of-the-population-council/) the Population Council, went on to chair Nixon’s population commission. He spent Rockefeller Foundation money on research and education about contraception.
